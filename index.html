Can mealworms interact with expanded polystyrene, and what happens when their frass is added to soil? A student project records the observations—and marks the questions that still need better evidence.

01 · The question

From foam to frass?

The project explored whether yellow mealworm larvae (Tenebrio molitor) would feed on EPS foam and whether different frass amendments coincided with plant growth in a small pot comparison.

The research question

What can a classroom experiment tell us?

Mealworms have been studied for their interaction with polystyrene. This project adds a local observation: it records larval survival, material collected, and early growth measurements from three plants. It cannot establish chemical breakdown or fertilizer safety.

Project aims
  • 1Record mealworm survival and visible interaction with clean EPS foam.
  • 2Compare early plant measurements across plastic-fed frass, regular frass, and plain soil.
  • 3Separate observed measurements from chemical claims that need lab testing.
02 · The protocol

A two-part study

The project notes describe ambient rearing conditions and a fixed watering schedule. The PDF report contains sample-count conflicts, detailed below.

ISEPT 23 — OCT 7 · 14 DAYS

Larval feeding

Larvae were offered clean, unprinted EPS. Carrot slices (1.0 g) were added every 48 hours for moisture. After a 24-hour settling period, material was sifted every 48 hours with a 1 mm mesh.

Recorded harvest: 1.78 g dry frass
IIOCT 7 — OCT 28 · 21 DAYS

Plant observations

Three pots contained 500 g garden soil each. A and B each received 5 g of frass; C was the unamended control. Each pot received 50 mL distilled water on alternating days.

Height & leaves: days 0, 7, 14, 21

Group A · EPS-fed frass

500 g soil + 5 g frass from larvae fed EPS.

Group B · Regular frass

500 g soil + 5 g frass from larvae on a bran-based diet.

Group C · Control

500 g plain garden soil with no frass amendment.

Conditions in the project notesAmbient 27–30 °C · target 65% RH · one plant per pot · parallel watering

04 · What was seen

Field observations

These notes are qualitative descriptions from the project report. Root and leaf observations were not measured with laboratory instruments.

◌

Material texture

EPS-fed material was described as a fine, light gray-beige powder; regular frass as darker, coarser pellets. Appearance alone cannot identify plastic fragments or chemical composition.

✳

Group A plant

The report describes deep green leaves, a thicker stem, and branched roots at day 21. These observations refer to one plant in this group.

⌁

Group C plant

The report describes pale lower leaves, a slender stem, and sparser roots in the control pot. No chlorophyll or root-mass measurement was recorded.

05 · Data integrity

Before calling it definitive

The supplied abstract, extended report, and project parameters do not all agree. This page shows the field-log series supplied earlier and keeps the conflicts visible rather than blending the numbers.

Three source conflicts need confirmation against the original logbook

The extended report and abstract give different counts and endpoint heights. The project details supplied in chat should be checked against the original records before public release.

Larvae at startProject parameters and report table: 100. Report abstract and other sections: 200. Three deaths mean 97% survival from 100, or 98.5% from 200.
Day-21 heightProject parameters and extended report: 28.4 / 25.2 / 19.8 cm. Abstract: 24.5 / 18.2 / 12.1 cm (A / B / C).
EPS feeding rate0.635 mg per larva per day matches 1.78 g over 14 days if the starting count was 200; 100 larvae would give about 1.27 mg per starting larva per day.
How are the endpoint percentages calculated?

Using the 28.4 / 25.2 / 19.8 cm series, Group A is 43.43% taller than Group C at day 21; 14 vs 9 leaves is a 55.56% difference; and 4.8 vs 3.2 mm stem thickness is a 50.00% difference. With one plant per group, these are descriptive comparisons, not a replicated estimate of treatment effect. The 21-day absolute growth rates from the same series are about 1.114, 0.962 and 0.705 cm/day for A, B and C.

What the study cannot establish

The trial had one plant per treatment and no reported replication or statistical analysis. It did not measure soil N-P-K, pH, electrical conductivity, polymer chemistry, microplastics, flame retardants, or plant-tissue residues. Plant growth differences may have other explanations. Mass loss and visible chewing do not by themselves verify biodegradation or mineralization.

06 · Safety boundary

Experimental material only

Do not apply plastic-fed frass to edible crops, kitchen gardens, farms, or food production. This project did not chemically test its samples. Keep material contained and follow school laboratory guidance for handling and disposal. The report’s data do not establish that ornamental use is safe either.

07 · Next questions

Where research could go

The project report identifies useful follow-up work that would strengthen both the measurements and the safety picture.

01 · Replicate

More plants per group

Repeat each treatment in multiple pots and randomize pot positions.

02 · Analyze

Test the material

Use qualified laboratories to check polymer residues and chemical additives.

03 · Measure

Extend the growth window

Record dry mass, soil chemistry, flowering, and longer-term outcomes.

04 · Compare

Standardize the protocol

Resolve the starting larval count and repeat the feeding-rate calculation from raw records.
